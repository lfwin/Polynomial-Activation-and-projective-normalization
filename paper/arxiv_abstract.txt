What makes a deep network train? Its nonlinearity, and the normalization that keeps the signal in range. We study both, and their interaction, through one construction: polynomial activations paired with projective (L2) normalization. Starting point: a ten-layer fully-connected network with activation f(z) = z + lambda*z^3, projected onto the unit sphere. We separate the polynomial nonlinearity from the projective normalization and evaluate each against the activations and normalizers that current large language models use (ReLU, GELU, SiLU, the gated SwiGLU and GeGLU; BatchNorm, LayerNorm, RMSNorm), plus classical controls (tanh, identity) and learnable polynomial bases (Legendre, Chebyshev, KAN-style cubic B-splines). Experiments span MNIST and eight synthetic datasets, depths from 2 to 50 layers, polynomial orders 2-5, lambda in [1e-3, 1e-1], and five seeds. Three results emerge, all about the pair, not either alone. First, the polynomial nonlinearity is not competitive on raw accuracy: it trails GELU by 3.86 points at equal parameter count. Second, the projective normalization recovers 1.59 of those points with zero learned parameters -- it pins the per-layer activation norm at its initial value (1x) where ReLU's decays to 0.048x and a gated unit's collapses to 0. Third, the same normalization makes depth reachable: the projected polynomial tracks ReLU to within 2.14 points at depth ten and 0.29 points at depth thirty, whereas an unnormalized polynomial collapses to chance at depth 30; and a gated unit without normalization fails outright while the same unit trains at 97.98% once normalization is added. We report the failure boundary honestly: at depth 50 both designs collapse to about 10.28%, so projective normalization buys depth, not unbounded depth.
